# From research to clinical use

<span id="section-2-1"></span>

## The translation gap in reproductive medicine

Assisted reproduction is a field of rapid technological change and modest success rates. For many patients, particularly those of advanced reproductive age, each treatment cycle carries a substantial emotional, physical and financial cost. New methods for assessing embryos, predicting endometrial receptivity or estimating reproductive ageing therefore attract strong interest from clinics and patients alike. A major challenge is that several techniques offered as IVF “add-ons” have entered routine practice before robust evidence of benefit was available. Patients are often not clearly told how limited that evidence is, and the data on clinical outcomes do not support their routine use [2](references.md#ref-2). In its good practice recommendations on add-ons, ESHRE does not recommend time-lapse imaging for routine use to improve outcomes, because the available evidence does not show an improvement over conventional morphological assessment [3](references.md#ref-3). This is a reminder that technical sophistication does not by itself establish clinical value.

Rigorous validation is how the field avoids both errors: adopting methods that do not help patients, and discarding methods that could. For NESTOR, validation is also the point where the project’s two defining features, cross-border mobility and academia–industry cooperation, become most concrete.

<span id="section-2-2"></span>

## Why validation must work across borders

The NESTOR consortium spans three health systems. A method developed and evaluated at MUMC+ will only be useful to a clinic in Thessaloniki or Tartu if there is evidence that it performs acceptably there. Differences between sites can affect performance in ways that are not visible in a single-site study:

- Technical differences: time-lapse platforms, imaging intervals, focal planes, optics and image formats.

- Laboratory differences: culture media and conditions, fertilisation method, timing conventions and embryo-handling routines.

- Human differences: training and experience of embryologists, annotation habits and the grading systems in use.

- Population differences: patient age distribution, indications for treatment and referral patterns.

- Legal and organisational differences: national rules implementing the GDPR, ethics review procedures, ART legislation and institutional IT policies governing where data may be processed.

Cross-border validation turns these differences from hidden sources of error into documented and testable factors. A shared validation workflow, together with harmonised minimum practices ([Chapter 4](harmonisation.md)), allows partners to learn from each other’s data without requiring identical infrastructure.

## Why validation must work across sectors

Translating a data-driven method into care requires contributions that no single type of organisation holds. [Table 1](clinical-translation.md#table-1) summarises the complementary roles represented in NESTOR.

<span id="table-1"></span>

**Table 1. Complementary roles of NESTOR partner types in validation**

| Partner type | Contribution to validation | Examples in NESTOR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Universities (UT, AUT, UM, NTUA) | Study design, statistics and computational methods; research ethics expertise; access to academic clinical research environments. | UM hosting the embryo-analysis work at MUMC+; training of seconded researchers under T3.1. |
| IVF clinics (AN, MUMC+) | Patient data and images; clinical annotation and judgement; knowledge of the clinical workflow; end users of validated methods. | AN implementing harmonised data collection, including TLM images, under T3.4. |
| Technology SMEs (INFS, Celvia) | Software engineering and AI development; quality management systems; experience of CE marking and accredited laboratory services. | INFS carrying out the embryo image analysis; Celvia’s ISO 15189-accredited laboratory and CE-marked IVD tests. |
| Innovation campus (BMHC) | Valorisation, intellectual property and regulatory training. | Training on IP protection and IVDR applications in WP4. |
| Advisory Board | Independent expertise in privacy law, biomedical ethics, health economics and patient perspectives. | Guidance on data protection, ethics and patient engagement. |

Secondments are the mechanism that binds these roles together. When an industry researcher works inside an academic hospital, or an academic researcher works in a company that maintains a certified quality system, validation practices are transferred directly between sectors. Written SOPs such as these guidelines make that transfer durable once the secondment ends.

## From research to clinical use

Validation proceeds in stages. Each stage answers a different question and produces a different kind of evidence. Applicable legal requirements depend on the intended purpose and activity ([Table 2](clinical-translation.md#table-2)). Placing a study correctly on this pathway is essential for interpreting its findings and for knowing which obligations apply.

<span id="table-2"></span>

**Table 2. Stages from research method to clinical use**

| Stage | Question | Typical evidence | Main legal framework |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A. Exploratory research | Can the method identify or measure what it targets in existing data? | Internal evaluation, visual review, documented pipeline and model record. | Applicable GDPR Article 6 basis and Article 9 condition, research safeguards and ethics review. Assess device and AI Act scope for the intended purpose and activity; Stage A is not itself an exemption. |
| B. Multi-site retrospective validation | Does performance hold across clinics, platforms and populations? | External and site-held-out validation; harmonised annotation; inter-observer agreement. | As A, plus data-sharing, processing or joint-controllership agreements across institutions and countries. |
| C. Prospective clinical evaluation | Does the method work within the clinical workflow, and how does it affect decisions and outcomes? | Prospective studies, early clinical evaluation, randomised trials. | Clinical investigation (MDR) or performance study (IVDR) where applicable; ethics approval; national ART law. |
| D. Clinical use | Is the method safe, effective and controlled in routine care? | Technical documentation, clinical evaluation, QMS, risk management. | Applicable device conformity route or conditional in-house route; AI Act and SoHO requirements where applicable and in force. |
| E. Post-deployment monitoring | Does the method continue to perform as expected over time? | Performance monitoring, drift detection, incident reporting. | Applicable post-market surveillance and vigilance (MDR/IVDR) and AI Act monitoring requirements. |

The NESTOR embryo image-analysis study described in [Chapter 6](embryo-image-analysis.md) sits at Stage A. The workflow in [Chapter 5](practical-workflow.md) is designed so that the documentation produced at Stage A can be carried forward into Stages B and C without having to be reconstructed.
